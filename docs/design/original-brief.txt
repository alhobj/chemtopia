I want to build a chemistry learning game from scratch, slowly and thoughtfully. Your first task is to help me decide what the game should be—not to start implementing it.

Treat this as a collaborative design process in which I make all chemistry, educational, visual, and gameplay decisions. You may recommend options and explain tradeoffs, but do not silently choose for me.

PROJECT PREMISE

The game is for students aged 16–19 studying IB Diploma Programme Chemistry.

It should be a browser game that works on computers, phones, and tablets. The visual and exploration starting point is a 2D, top-down, tile-based pixel-art game inspired by Pokémon Red. This is a reference for discussion, not permission to copy its assets or assume its other mechanics.

Ideas I want to carry forward from an earlier game:
- A laboratory.
- Different map regions associated with different sections of the chemistry syllabus.
- Unlocking areas and laboratory equipment.
- Exploration and dialogue.

The details of these ideas still need my approval.

Chemistry should remain central. Some exploration and interaction with the environment are welcome, but students should not spend long stretches travelling, completing errands, or doing unrelated activities before reaching meaningful chemistry.

The game must be welcoming to students of any gender, including those who rarely or never play games. Do not assume familiarity with gaming conventions. Ask how we should make controls, instructions, navigation, and progression understandable.

We will build from scratch in a new folder, probably in a new Git repository. The previous project may be available for inspiration, but its files will not necessarily be in their old location. Do not assume you can find it, import it, or modify it.

YOUR FIRST RESPONSE: A LARGE DESIGN QUESTIONNAIRE

Start with one substantial questionnaire, grouped by topic, using Markdown checkboxes.

Do not start coding, scaffold the project, create files, install dependencies, or create a repository before I have answered the questionnaire and approved your interpretation of my answers.

Use as many questions as necessary to establish a thoughtful foundation. Make the questions concrete and understandable without technical knowledge. Avoid repetitive questions or unnecessary choices.

For each question:
- State whether I should select one option or any number.
- Offer useful, distinct options.
- Include “Undecided—discuss with me” where appropriate.
- Allow a written alternative or additional notes.
- Leave boxes unchecked.
- Explain unfamiliar terms briefly.
- Where a decision has substantial consequences, explain the tradeoffs briefly without steering me into a default.

Give every question a stable identifier so I can answer by number instead of copying the whole questionnaire.

Distinguish the few things I have already specified from details that remain open. Do not make me reconfirm the exact same requirements, but ask about their implementation and boundaries.

Include the following topics, plus anything important that is missing:

1. EDUCATIONAL GOALS AND SYLLABUS
- Which syllabus version and examination years to target.
- Standard Level, Higher Level, or both.
- Which chemistry topics to include in the first version and eventually.
- How map regions should relate to syllabus topics and their connections.
- Learning, practice, revision, assessment, or a combination.
- Conceptual understanding, calculations, practical skills, data interpretation, and scientific reasoning.
- How closely terminology, notation, units, and question styles should follow IB expectations.
- Which sources should establish chemistry accuracy.
- How I will review and approve chemistry content.
- How to handle simplifications and the limits of scientific models.

2. THE CENTRAL PLAY EXPERIENCE
- What students actually do most often.
- What a typical short session should feel like.
- The balance between chemistry, exploration, dialogue, and laboratory work.
- Whether chemistry should appear in environmental interactions, explicit challenges, experiments, or other formats.
- Expected session length.
- How much freedom students have to choose their next activity.
- How quickly they should reach meaningful chemistry.

HOW TO HANDLE MY ANSWERS

After I answer:
1. Summarise what I have explicitly chosen.
2. Identify unanswered questions, contradictions, dependencies, and decisions that need more discussion.
3. Ask focused follow-up questions without filling gaps yourself.
4. Present a proposed design premise for my approval.
5. Do not begin implementation until I approve that premise and the first small development step.

An unanswered question means “undecided,” not permission to choose. If I answer only part of the questionnaire, retain the remaining questions as open.

If the questionnaire or my answers are too long for one message, keep stable numbering and let me answer in sections. Do not turn partial answers into an approved design.

DECISION AUTHORITY DURING DEVELOPMENT

You may make routine technical decisions without asking, provided they do not change the approved experience, chemistry, appearance, scope, cost, privacy, or compatibility.

Examples include ordinary code organisation, internal naming, straightforward refactoring, and fixes that restore already approved behaviour.

Always ask before adding or changing:
- Chemistry content, examples, explanations, questions, or scientific simplifications.
- Learning objectives or syllabus coverage.
- Gameplay mechanics or progression rules.
- Regions, equipment, characters, dialogue, or story.
- Visual direction or meaningful interface behaviour.
- Rewards, penalties, difficulty, timers, or unlocking conditions.
- Features that affect student data, accounts, paid services, or publishing.

You may propose concrete options and recommend one, with reasons. Wait for my choice before implementing it.

If a technical choice affects the student experience or limits future approved requirements, explain the effect and ask. Do not use “technical decision” as a reason to settle a design question.

Use clearly identified placeholders when needed. Do not invent chemistry or game content to make a prototype feel complete.

WORKING RECORD AND DEVELOPMENT PROCESS

After approval to create project files, keep a concise, persistent record in the repository of:
- Approved design and requirements.
- Approved chemistry scope and sources.
- Decisions and their reasons.
- Open questions.
- Deferred ideas.
- The current development step and its acceptance criteria.
- Completed work and relevant verification.

Clearly distinguish approved decisions from suggestions. Do not promote a suggestion into a requirement.

Develop in small, reviewable increments. Before each increment, describe the intended result and surface any decisions that need my input. Once that increment is approved, complete its routine implementation and appropriate checks without asking about every line of code.

At the end of each increment, explain what changed, how I can try it, what was checked, and what remains undecided. Let me review it before moving to the next feature.

Preserve the old project. Do not delete, overwrite, copy from, or modify it without my explicit permission.

Start now with the grouped checkbox questionnaire. Take your time with the questions. Do not implement anything yet.
